# 2026-10-27: the formal core (the shared structure, and two theorems about it)

Status labels follow [STANDARDS.md](../STANDARDS.md). A proof is reasoning, so each theorem below is labeled INFERRED. Where the same proof appears in a primary source that was read, the entry says so. This entry answers PROGRAM.md section 3 ("the abstract structure is not yet written down. That is the first formal task") and section 9, open question 1. It uses only as much structure as the two theorems need.

## In short

- **The structure** (section 1): items that use one another, payees credited for items, a fixed budget of a scarce resource, a pricing rule that divides the budget, and anchors. An anchor is an input the contributor cannot fake for less than it pays. Each piece has a mind reading and an economy reading.
- **Theorem G, grounding** (section 2): suppose a pricing rule sees only the pattern of relations, with no anchor. Then a ring that copies a closed part of the graph gets exactly what the original gets, and with a fixed budget, k copies take k/(k+1) of it. So no such rule can separate genuine work from a wash. This is Cheng and Friedman's 2005 impossibility theorem for reputation systems, restated. **Not new.**
- **Theorem S, splitting** (section 3): a per-identity floor lets one contributor take nearly the whole budget by splitting into many identities, whatever it actually contributed. Paying per unit of measured output gives a splitter nothing, and it is the only rule that resists both splitting and merging. This is the proportional-rule characterization from the bankruptcy literature (O'Neill 1982; de Frutos 1999; Ju, Miyagawa and Sakai 2007), plus Yokoo et al.'s 2005 result for coalitional games. **Not new.**
- **What it buys ETM** (section 5): G1 and X9 become theorems with stated hypotheses, borrowed from economics and computer science. They bear on "a mind is an economy" only where a mind is shown to have the structure. Artificial minds built as economies have it by construction. For natural minds it is an open question.
- **One correction it forces** (Remark G3): "conserved credit" means two different things. A normalized score (PageRank, the kind M1 tested) does not resist pumping. Transfer conservation does: every endorsement costs the endorser what it gives, and new credit enters only at anchors. But then the anchor is doing the work. X3 has to say which kind it means.

## 1. The abstract structure

Eight definitions. Nothing else is used.

**D1. Items and use.** There is a finite set V of items and a set E of use edges: (u, v) in E means u used, cited, or built on v. Optionally each edge has a weight w(u, v) > 0, and each item has observable attributes a(v) such as size, age, or a content summary.

**D2. Payees and principals.** Each item is credited to a payee, written own(v). A payee is the unit the rule pays as one. Behind the payees are principals, the parties who actually benefit. The rule does not see which payees share a principal.

**D3. Observation.** O = (V, E, w, a, own) is everything the rule sees. Owner-disjointness, as C1 uses it, is part of O, since it is computed from own.

**D4. Budget and pricing rule.** B > 0 is the budget of a scarce resource for one period. A pricing rule P maps an observation O to a credit p(v) >= 0 for each item. P is *budget-conserving* if the credits always sum to B. A payee's credit is the sum of its items' credits.

**D5. Anchor-free (relational) rules.** An *isomorphism* between two observations renames items and payees while preserving every edge, every weight, every attribute, and which items share a payee. P is *anchor-free* if renaming never changes a verdict: when O' is a renamed copy of O, every item gets in O' the credit its original got in O. This is Cheng and Friedman's "symmetric" ("a symmetric function is one which is invariant under a renaming of the nodes (i.e. it depends only on the edge structure of the graph)", their section 2). It formalizes PROGRAM.md's "relational" rule when there is no outside input. A rule can break this symmetry only by reading something that is not in the pattern. Whatever it reads is an anchor.

**D6. Anchors and independence.** An *anchor* is an input to the rule that a given contributor cannot produce or change for less than the reward it would unlock. Examples: a seed set of items whose standing was settled before the contributor arrived, a reward from the environment, a payment from outside, energy spent, a trusted clock. A rule that reads an anchor only has to be invariant under renamings that also preserve the anchor. A use relation is *independent* of a contributor if it is an anchor itself, or if it traces back along use edges to an anchor through items the contributor does not control. That is the precise sense of "relations that bottom out outside the contributor's control." T5's "independence is a price" draws the same line in terms of cost.

**D7. Closed parts, genuine work, and the wash.** A part C of the observation is *closed* if three things hold: no use edge joins C to the rest in either direction, no payee owns items both inside and outside C, and C contains no anchor. Genuineness is a fact about the world, not about the observation. Work is genuine if its uses did something outside the system. It is a wash if its uses were produced by the contributor for itself. A *copy-wash* of C is a set of new items under new payees that a renaming maps onto C (same edges, weights, attributes, and payee pattern), all of whose uses the contributor made.

**D8. Profiles, splitting, merging** (for Theorem S). A profile lists payees i = 1..n with measured outputs m_i >= 0 and total M > 0. A division rule assigns payments x_i >= 0 with x_1 + ... + x_n = B. A principal *splits* a payee by replacing it with several payees whose outputs add up to the original. *Merging* is the reverse. A rule is *split-proof* if splitting never raises the total paid to the pieces, and *merge-proof* if merging never raises the total paid to the group that merged.

**The dictionary.** These are the PROGRAM.md section 2 rows, fixed here so each theorem reads on both sides.

| Structure | Mind side | Economy side |

| item (D1) | a memory, thought, rule, or module | a contribution, asset, crate, or product |
| use edge (D1) | recalling or using an item; one rule setting up another; a note citing a note | depending on, building on, buying, citing |
| payee (D2) | the unit the mind's allocation counts as one: a note, a rule, a category | an account, wallet, or identity |

**Bridge assumption, mind side (INFERRED).** On the economy side a principal chooses to split or to wash. On the mind side nothing chooses. There, "profitable" has to mean "favored by the allocation," and favored variants spread only where variants arise and are selected, as with parasite rules in Holland's system or cheating cells in X5. Every mind-side reading below depends on this assumption.

## 2. Theorem G: anchor-free rules cannot see a copy

**Assumptions.**
- G-a (no anchor). The rule sees only the observation and is anchor-free (D5).
- G-b (free creation). The contributor can create any number of items, use edges among its own items, attribute values, and payees, at no cost.
- G-c (fixed budget). The rule is budget-conserving (D4). Only part (ii) uses this.

**Theorem G.** Let C be a closed part of an observation O (D7), however genuine. Let O_k be O together with k copy-washes C_1, ..., C_k of C. Each copy has fresh payees and is disjoint from everything else. Then, under P:
- (i) every item of C receives the same credit as its image in each copy;
- (ii) the copies together receive k/(k+1) of the credit that goes to C and its copies, so if C is all of O, the genuine work's credit falls from B to B/(k+1);
- (iii) no anchor-free rule separates genuine contribution from a wash on any class of worlds that contains these constructions. On them, the rule's output cannot even be correlated with genuineness.

**Proof.** For each j, let s_j swap every item of C with its image in C_j, swap their payees the same way, and leave everything else fixed. C and C_j are closed, so no edge and no shared payee joins either one to the rest. So s_j preserves every edge, weight, attribute, and payee pattern of O_k: it is a renaming of O_k onto itself. Because P is anchor-free, p(s_j(v)) = p(v) for every item v. That is, each item of C has the same credit as its image in C_j, which proves (i). Summing over C shows that each copy's total equals C's total. Call it T. Then C and its copies receive (k+1)T, and the copies receive kT of that. If C is all of O, budget conservation gives (k+1)T = B, which proves (ii). For (iii), compare two worlds: in one, C is genuine and C_1 is a wash; in the other, the roles are reversed. Both produce the same observation O_k, so the rule gives the same output in both. Separation would require each copy to get less than C, which (i) rules out. QED.

This is the proof of Cheng and Friedman's (2005) Theorem 1, "There is no symmetric sybilproof nontrivial reputation function." Their construction is "a disjoint union of two copies of G." There are three translations here. Reputation becomes price. Their sybils become any ring of payees, real and distinct or not, because the observation is the same either way. And a budget is added, for part (ii). Literature checked: primary text read 2026-10-27.

**Corollary G1 (free credit is worse; INFERRED).** Suppose credit is not conserved and grows with incoming use (raw citation counts, unauthenticated access counts). Then a closed ring can raise its credit without bound by adding uses among its own items. Cheng and Friedman (2006) give the in-degree case: "a user may increase his indegree to any desired value by creating sybils."

**Proposition G2 (the anchored escape; known).** Let S be a nonempty set of anchored items that the contributor cannot join or change. Let P pass credit only along use paths that start in S, such as a flow or a random walk from S. Then every closed part without an anchor gets zero credit, because no path from S enters it. This is the "asymmetric" route in Cheng and Friedman (2005, section 4.2): "asymmetric reputation functions may assume that some specified nodes are trusted, and propagate trust from those nodes." Their Theorem 4 gives conditions under which no sybil strategy raises a node's value. It is also the design of TrustRank (Gyöngyi, Garcia-Molina and Pedersen 2004), which propagates trust from a seed set chosen by human experts. **The price:** a wash can still get credit through use edges that run from anchored items into it. The anchor moves the attack onto those edges. It does not end the attack. C2's reading, item 4, records one such path on crates.io: a crate counts as an independent user of d if it reaches d through a single intermediate crate under another owner. The Sybil-defense literature on social networks (SybilGuard, SybilLimit, SybilRank) bounds the damage by the number of such edges. That literature is standard and was not checked here.

**Remark G3 (two kinds of conserved credit; this corrects X3).**
- *Budget conservation* (normalization): the rule's output sums to B. PageRank is of this kind, and it is the "conserved credit" M1 tested. It does not resist pumping. Cheng and Friedman (2006) find "PageRank is extremely manipulable, even with simple strategies using a small number of sybils," and by Theorem G(ii) a copy-ring takes its full share.
- *Transfer conservation*: credit moves only as transfers along use edges. Each transfer debits the payer what it credits the payee, and new credit enters only at anchors. This is Miller and Drexler's local conservation and the money of Baum's Hayek machine (PRIOR-ART). Under it, a group of payees changes its total only through flows across its boundary and through anchor rewards, because transfers inside the group cancel. Qi et al. (2026, Appendix C) use exactly this decomposition. Their Lemma 3 proves that a colluding coalition is removed in finite time if all of its surviving sub-coalitions lose wealth externally (read in part; see section 4). So a closed wash ring cannot grow at all. But closed genuine work cannot grow either, because with no anchor nothing does. Transfer conservation therefore does not separate genuine work from a wash on its own. What it does is make anchors the only source of credit, which turns every rule into an anchored one (G2). That is the formal reason the Hayek machine needed both conservation and money from outside (X1, X4).
- *Consequence for X3 (INFERRED).* The restated X3 predicts that "under an attack that adds free credit ... conserved-credit rankings do not [degrade]." On the literature above, it should fail for budget-normalized credit such as PageRank. It can hold for transfer conservation, but there the anchor, not the conservation, does the separating. X3 should name which kind of conservation it means, and STANDARDS requires this to be recorded next to X3 as a counterexample.

**Where the proof is weak, and where a stronger G1 fails.**
1. *The attacker must copy a whole closed part, fake users included.* If the genuine work sits inside a large connected economy, copying it means copying that whole economy. Theorem G is a worst case for an attacker who can afford a full copy, and G-b makes the copy free. Against a smaller wash, an anchor-free rule can sometimes separate. "Pay only items in the largest connected part" is anchor-free, and it pays nothing to a closed ring smaller than what it imitates. Noesis's built interior defenses also catch cyclic self-citation (Noesis `ARCHITECTURE.md:154-155` at `6a8ebcaf`). But even the largest-part rule falls to edges the wash creates for free: a wash that cites into the economy joins its connected part. So a rule that resists has to key on incoming use, and that brings back the question of who produced the use. What anchor-free rules can do is defend by mass. The wash must be smaller than what it copies, which is an honest-majority assumption measured in graph size (INFERRED; not proved here).
2. *G-b is false in every real system.* Items cost content, fees, deposits, and time. Theorem G does not put a price on a copy. It says that the cost of the copy, or an anchor, is the only defense there is. This is where the formal core hands over to T5: independence is a price.
3. *Static.* There is one observation and one verdict. If a rule uses history and the history is just more observation, a patient wash copies the history too. If a trusted clock stamps the history, the clock is an anchor and G-a fails, as it should.
4. *What G1 says beyond the theorem.* G1 reads: "relational prices track worth only when the relations come from independent minds." (a) The proof shows that some anchor is necessary, not that minds are. Any input the contributor cannot copy for less than the reward will do: energy, physical delivery as in host sanctions, a trusted clock, revenue. (b) The proof does not show that anchored prices track worth, and G2's price says they can be laundered. (c) It covers full copies only (point 1). So the proved part of G1 is: *no separation without an anchor, against a full-copy wash.*
5. *C1 is covered by the theorem.* In C1, the `value_v5` flow and both count nulls read only the observation, owner-disjointness included, so they are anchor-free in the sense of D5. A copy of a closed part under fresh owners would score exactly as the original does. So C1's support for G1 is real but holds only in an honest world, as C1's limit 3 already says. Theorem G is the reason that limit cannot be relaxed without an anchor.

**Status:** parts (i) to (iii) are proved under G-a and G-b, with G-c also needed for (ii). Labeled INFERRED, since it is a proof; the same construction is in the checked primary source. Prior art: a restatement (section 4).

## 3. Theorem S: floors pay splitters; paying per output does not

**Assumptions.**
- S-a (identity-blind). The rule sees only the list of measured outputs. It treats any reordering of the payees the same way and cannot see which payees share a principal.
- S-b (additive measurement). Splitting a payee into pieces, or merging payees, changes no other payee's measured output, and the pieces' outputs add up to the whole. Splitting creates no measured output and destroys none.
- S-c (free identities, divisible output). A principal can create payees at no cost and can divide its output into any number of positive pieces.
- S-d (fixed budget, no negative payments). The payments sum to B and none is negative.

A **floor rule** with floor F (0 < F < B) is any rule of the following kind. Let n+ be the number of payees with positive output. Whenever n+ times F <= B, every payee with positive output gets at least F. The **proportional rule** pays x_i = B m_i / M.

**Theorem S.**
- (a) Under a floor rule, consider a principal with any positive output, facing n other payees with positive output. It can split into k = floor(B/F) - n payees (when k >= 1) and receive k F > B - (n + 1) F, no matter how small its output is.
- (b) Under the proportional rule, the total paid to any group of payees is B times the group's share of total output. Splitting and merging change nothing.
- (c) Consider any rule that satisfies S-a, S-b and S-d on every profile, with any number of payees. If it is both split-proof and merge-proof, it is the proportional rule.

**Proof.**

(a) After the split there are n + k = floor(B/F) payees with positive output, and floor(B/F) times F <= B. So the floor binds, and each of the k pieces gets at least F. Their total is at least k F = (floor(B/F) - n) F > (B/F - 1 - n) F = B - (n + 1) F.

(b) A group with total output m_G receives B m_G / M. By S-b, splitting or merging inside the group leaves both m_G and M unchanged.

(c) Splitting a payee and then merging the pieces back returns the original profile. So split-proofness and merge-proofness together give equality: no split or merge changes the total paid to the payees involved. Call this *neutrality*.
- Step 1 (only own output and the total matter). Take a profile and a payee i, and merge all the other payees into one payee z. By neutrality, z gets exactly what the others got together. By S-d, in both profiles i gets B minus that amount. So i's payment equals its payment in the two-payee profile (m_i, M - m_i), which by S-a depends only on m_i and M. Call it g(m_i, M).
- Step 2 (additivity). For x, y >= 0 with x + y <= M, take the three-payee profile (x, y, M - x - y) and merge the first two payees. Neutrality gives g(x, M) + g(y, M) = g(x + y, M). Setting x = y = 0 gives g(0, M) = 0: a payee with no output gets nothing. This follows from the axioms; it is not assumed.
- Step 3 (linearity). As a function of its first argument, g is additive and nonnegative, so it is nondecreasing: g(x + y) = g(x) + g(y) >= g(x). Additivity gives g(rM, M) = r g(M, M) for every rational r in [0, 1]. Monotonicity then pins down every real point between rationals. So g(t, M) = (t / M) g(M, M).
- Step 4 (the constant). Summing over payees gives g(M, M) = B. Hence x_i = B m_i / M. QED.

This is the standard argument for the bankruptcy (claims) problem. De Frutos (1999, Theorem 1) characterizes the proportional rule by merging-splitting-proofness, efficiency and non-negativity. Ju, Miyagawa and Sakai (2007, Corollary 11) weaken this to merging and splitting by pairs plus a one-sided bound. Both results are as reported by Ju, Miyagawa and Sakai, whose paper was read; de Frutos was not. They also show that S-a need not be assumed, because identity-blindness follows from the other axioms.

**Worked number (INFERRED arithmetic, abstract rule).** Take a 1% floor (F = B/100) and 10 honest payees. A splitter with any positive output splits into 90 payees and takes 90% of the budget. The VibeSwap reference distributor has a floor of this kind: `LAWSON_FAIRNESS_FLOOR = 100` basis points, a 1% share for each participant (`contracts/incentives/ShapleyDistributor.sol:98` at `243280eb`, VERIFIED). Its code comment records "Found by adversarial search: 200/200 rounds showed profitable sybil splitting" (`:295`; VERIFIED as a comment, the search itself REPORTED). At `243280eb`, the floor reverts unless a Sybil guard is set, and it pays only verified unique identities (`:894-924`, revert at `:920`, check at `:923`; VERIFIED). That fix moves the defense from the price to identity, and Douceur (2002) shows identity needs a trusted authority or resource tests.

**Remarks.**
- *Each half of (c) rules out a family of rules.* Floors fail split-proofness, and so do concave rules such as square-root (quadratic) voting, because the root of a sum is less than the sum of the roots. Bennett, Vander Vos, Le and Belenkiy (2026) prove that any voting rule based only on wallet balance, on a permissionless chain, gives a splitting attacker power at least linear in its holdings. Under the best split, concave rules become linear in the limit. That paper was read at abstract level only. Convex rules (winner take all; payment by the square of output) fail merge-proofness instead, because they reward collusion. Proportional pricing sits exactly between the two families.
- *Fixed versus variable population.* With a fixed set of payees, rules that treat payees by identity (equal division, a floor) can survive any reallocation of outputs among them. Ju, Miyagawa and Sakai's Theorem 1 writes every reallocation-proof rule as "a 'priority part,' which may treat agents differently based on their identities but ignores differences among their characteristics, and an 'additive part'". The floor fails only because splitting changes the number of payees. Its flaw is a Sybil problem, not a pricing problem within a fixed population.
- *The two theorems compose (INFERRED).* Theorem S pushes the price onto measured output and leaves no defense in the identity layer. Theorem G says output measured from relations alone, with no anchor, can be copied. So a rule can defend against cheating in only two ways. It can defend by identity, which needs identities that are costly to fake (Douceur's trusted authority or resource tests, or VibeSwap's guard). Or it can defend by output, which needs an anchor in the measurement. With free identities and anchor-free measurement, it has no defense at all. This is the lesson of TRANSFERS E-4, stated formally: a bound stabilizes only when it is attached to "a unit of verified output (X9) or an independent mind (G1)." It was not found stated this way in the sources read. It combines two known results directly and is not claimed as new.

**Where the proof is weak.**
1. *S-b carries the theorem, and real credit measures often break it.* Shapley values computed over agents are not additive across identities. In Yokoo et al. (2005, Examples 1 and 2), an agent with two skills puts each skill under its own identity and raises its Shapley share from 1/2 to 2/3 (1/3 per identity). Synergy-based measures, concave transforms applied per identity, and per-identity damping break S-b too. Yokoo et al.'s cure is the per-output one: "Applying any solution concept to the skills directly is robust to false names, collusion, and any combinations thereof" (their Theorem 3). It leaves one manipulation open: hiding skills. This entry did not check whether the weights in VibeSwap's Shapley distributor are additive across identities.
2. *S-c is false wherever identities cost something.* With a cost c per identity, floor splitting pays only while F > c, and minimum output units cap the number of pieces. Theorem S(a) is the limit when identities are free.
3. *(c) needs both merge-proofness and a variable population.* Drop either one and other rules qualify (remarks 1 and 2).
4. *Theorem S says nothing about whether measured output is real.* That question belongs to Theorem G.

**Status:** parts (a) to (c) are proved under S-a to S-d. Labeled INFERRED, since it is a proof; part (c) matches the checked characterization in Ju, Miyagawa and Sakai. Prior art: a restatement (section 4).

## 5. What this does and does not establish

1. **What it establishes.** Theorems G and S hold for any system that has the structure of section 1 and meets the stated assumptions. Under the dictionary, each one reads on both sides at once, which is what PROGRAM.md section 3 asked of the formal route. ETM contributes the vocabulary and the scope statements here, not the mathematics. (INFERRED, as proofs; literature checked.)
2. **What it does not establish: T1.** A theorem about a structure says nothing about whether minds have that structure. Theorem G is also not specifically economic. It holds for any renaming-invariant function on a graph (reputation systems, web ranking, citation metrics), and its main part never uses the budget. So G cannot distinguish "a mind is an economy" from "a mind is a graph with a ranking rule" (PROGRAM.md, open question 2). Theorem S does use the economic ingredients, a fixed budget divided among claimants, so it is the more economic of the two.
3. **Evidence that an economy has the structure (strong where the economy was designed).**
   - Reward pools are built with a fixed budget, payees, and a pricing rule: VibeSwap's distributor (VERIFIED code at `243280eb`) and Noesis's value layer. The Noesis layer is built in the reference code but is not on the live path: "`value_v5..v8` have no caller in the runtime or the RPC" (Noesis `ARCHITECTURE.md:143-144` at `6a8ebcaf`, VERIFIED).

   - Copy-blindness has been observed. A simulated competent acyclic wash showed "0.0% separation" from genuine collaboration (Noesis `ARCHITECTURE.md:150-152` at `6a8ebcaf`; VERIFIED as a recorded result, simulation not re-run here). PageRank link farms show the same (Cheng and Friedman 2006; checked).
   - Anchors help: in C1-d, owner-independent use forecast better in 4 of 4 years (measured). That was an honest world, and that anchor is free to fake, so it works as an anchor only against honest noise.
   - Floors get split: the VibeSwap comment (VERIFIED as a comment; search REPORTED) and DAO voting (checked at abstract level).
4. **Evidence that a mind has the structure (partial, and mostly by construction).**
   - *Artificial minds built as economies:* Holland's classifier systems (parasites), the Hayek machine (outside money, conservation, bankruptcy), and Qi et al. (2026) (language-model agents, environmental reward, Lemma 3). They have the structure because they were built with it. They show that a mind *can* be built this way and that the anchor matters there. They are not evidence that minds in general work this way.
   - *The authors' AI memory:* items, citations, and a fixed context budget. Citation and recall are uncorrelated (REPORTED, not checked). In M1, conserved and free credit tied on an honest link graph (measured). No one has tried a copy-wash on it.
   - *Belief as a budget (consistency only).* Probability inside a mind sums to one, so it is a conserved budget divided among hypotheses. Ju, Miyagawa and Sakai's Corollary 8 says that, with at least 3 states, Bayes rule is the only updating rule that satisfies pairwise reallocation-proofness, efficiency, no award for null, and non-negativity (read). Here reallocation-proofness means "the updated probability of a given event S depends on the initial belief over the states in S only through the total probability that the initial belief puts on S as a whole." In other words, how a hypothesis is carved into sub-hypotheses does not change its weight. Human judgment shows partition dependence (Fox, Ratner and Lieb 2005; listing checked, text not read). Allocation that leans toward even shares across whatever categories are presented acts like a per-category floor, which is Theorem S(a) on the mind side. Three caveats. Belief is not in the PROGRAM.md dictionary, and adding it now, after seeing the fit, makes this consistency, not evidence. The floor reading is INFERRED. The effect's robustness is mixed (REPORTED).
   - *Human minds, otherwise:* attention is scarce (Simon 1971; standard). The standard account of credit assignment along chains of events, with reward as the anchor, is temporal-difference learning and dopamine; it was not checked here. Nothing known to this program says a brain's allocation is conserved in either sense of Remark G3, and no wash with the copy structure has been identified in a brain.
   - **Missing:** (1) a mind-side system, not built as an economy, in which the assumptions (a conserved budget, an anchor-free or anchored rule, additive measurement) are checked against the system rather than assumed; (2) a pre-registered transfer from these theorems to a mind. One concrete candidate, not yet registered: in an AI memory, take a keep rule that reads only links (anchor-free). A self-citing cluster that copies the link pattern of a used cluster should be kept at the used cluster's rate, while a rule fed by task outcomes (anchored) should keep it at zero. Hit and miss criteria must be written before any run.
5. **Net.** The formal route now has two theorems, both borrowed, and labeled as borrowed. T1 is unchanged (open). Three things change. G1's proved part is narrower and sharper than its wording: an anchor is necessary against a full copy, and minds are one kind of anchor. X9 is a known theorem with one load-bearing assumption (additive measurement), which real Shapley-based credit can violate. And X3 must say which kind of conservation it means.
